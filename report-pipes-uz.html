Systemic Water Supply Problems & Modular Pipe Plants

Economic justification for replacing worn-out trunk mains and transitioning to local production of composite pipes. All calculations are in US dollars.

Analysis Period2021–2026
StatusPreliminary (analytical)
ScopeUzbekistan (National)

1. Executive Summary

Drinking water losses in Uzbekistan's networks reach 35–55%. This means that every second or third cubic meter of treated water does not reach the consumer. At a tariff of approximately $0.19/m³, economic losses amount to hundreds of millions of dollars annually.

Traditional materials (cast iron, steel, concrete, PE) have documented failure mechanisms that manifest faster in Uzbekistan's conditions than in Europe. Composite pipes with local production provide a threefold reduction in material cost and a sixfold reduction in weight, radically changing logistics and installation.

Key figure: With 12 modular plants operating (1,200 km of pipes per year), the total annual saving for the country exceeds $520 million — through reduced pipe costs, logistics, installation, and water conservation.

2. Current Drinking Water Losses

2.1 Regional Context

According to the Eurasian Development Bank (2024), the wear rate of water supply networks in Central Asia reaches 80%, with water losses during transmission of up to 55%. The annual financing gap for the regional water sector is $2 billion.

2.2 Uzbekistan: Confirmed Data

Government target: by 2030, water losses must be reduced from 35% to 25%.

Actual data for Tashkent region (2024): according to "Uzsuvtaminot," district-level losses range from 19.89% (Bustonlik) to 44.18% (Okhangaron district). The average for "Tashkent suv taminoti" is 38.98%.

Link between losses and materials: physical leaks through pinholes, cracks, and joint failures (cast iron, steel, concrete, substandard PE). Pipe replacement does not eliminate commercial losses, but eliminating physical leaks is a direct saving of water in cubic meters, convertible into dollars.

3. Technical Failure Mechanisms of Traditional Pipes

3.1 Cast Iron

Mechanism: Brittle fracture under ground movement (frost heave, subsidence). In St. Petersburg, 53% of networks are grey cast iron, the most vulnerable to frost heave. In Dimitrovgrad, a cast iron main suffered its seventh rupture in a year. In Uzbekistan, Regional Development Fund projects state directly: "due to worn-out cast iron pipes, water lines frequently enter emergency states."

Conclusion: The service life of cast iron networks laid in the 1960s–1980s is exhausted. This is not a "risk" but an established condition.

3.2 Steel

Mechanism: Corrosion, pinholes, internal scaling. Steel "leaks quietly" for years until pressure drops enough for the problem to become obvious. Over 55% of Uzbekistan's pipelines have a service life exceeding 25 years; a significant portion is Soviet-era steel.

Conclusion: A steel pipe that has served 40 years in Uzbek water is not subject to "diagnostics with life extension" — only replacement.

3.3 Concrete and Reinforced Concrete

Mechanism: Chloride-induced reinforcement corrosion and cement paste leaching. In Uzbekistan's saline soils, chlorides penetrate the pipe body, corrode the prestressing wire, and the pipe loses load-bearing capacity without visible leaks. In the US, asbestos-cement pipes (a related material) in San Diego produced 440 failures over a decade.

Conclusion: A concrete pipe may look normal on the outside for years while already being destroyed inside. This makes it the most dangerous material in terms of sudden failure.

3.4 PE (Polyethylene)

Mechanisms:

  • Organic migration: PE releases phenols, quinones, and antioxidant degradation products. In Denmark, it has been confirmed that Irganox 1010 concentration can reach 0.2–0.3 mg/L.
  • Unconfirmed service life: "50-year" claims are based on laboratory extrapolation, not real-world data.
  • Sensitivity to disinfection: Chlorine dioxide at just 0.15 mg/L reduces PE pipe service life to 10 years instead of 50.
  • Machine maintenance: Failure to perform monthly calibrations causes anti-vandal additives to migrate to the inner wall in contact with drinking water.

Conclusion: A large-diameter PE pipe on a trunk drinking water main is not "savings" but a deferred water hammer and organic migration.

3.5 Continuous Winding Fiberglass (Specific Case)

Mechanism: The continuous winding machine was originally designed for gravity-flow and sewer systems. When applied to a pressurized drinking water main, the following are absent: a certified inner liner, ring strength calculation for water hammer, and batch traceability.

Evidence: In Khavaст district (Syrdarya), a fiberglass main developed leaks, and "Uzsuvtaminot" officially acknowledged that the pipes "cannot withstand network pressure."

4. Composite Pipes: The Only Material Covering All Failure Mechanisms

Glass-reinforced plastic (GRP per ISO 23856) and basalt-composite pipes:

  • Do not corrode — no metal, no cathodic protection or coating required.
  • Resistant to ground movement — elastic, absorbing up to 40% of water hammer pressure. The bell-and-spigot joint with elastomeric seal is standardized under ISO 8639 specifically for service under ground movement. The coupler joint has two sealing surfaces and "plays" — it is the source of leakage, not the bell.
  • Sanitarily safe — smooth inner surface, no corrosion products released.
  • Confirmed 50+ year service life — GRP manufacturers conduct four types of long-term testing.
  • Compliant with ISO 23856:2021 for drinking water, DN 50–4000.
  • 4–8 times lighter than steel, 1/5 the weight of cast iron, 1/10 the weight of reinforced concrete.
  • Already produced in Uzbekistan (Ecobasalt Group, Jizzakh region: $19.7 million investment, 29% reduced energy consumption, 5.24-year payback; Basalt Uzbekistan).

4.1 Repairability of Composite Pipes

Lamination method: Layers of glass or basalt fiber with resin are applied to the damaged area. Repair can be performed both externally and internally (for DN700 and DN1000), without full trench excavation.

Local replacement method: Cutting out the damaged section and installing a repair coupling. The pipe is cut with standard tools, requiring no welding or heavy equipment.

Comparison: Cast iron requires a crane and caulking; steel requires gas welding and hot work. Composite repair takes hours, not days, and creates no risk of new damage. For drinking water supply, this means minimizing the time water is in contact with the external environment and reducing the risk of secondary contamination.

Repair economics: Lamination — ~10–15% of the cost of a new meter; local replacement — ~20–30%; cast iron repair — 50–70% plus the risk of repeated failure.

7. Institutional Problems and Confirmed Corruption Cases

7.1 Established Cases

  • Piskent district, Tashkent region (2026): 14,800 m of pipes fictitiously claimed, worth 3.0784 billion soum (≈$244,000). Contract — 3.9312 billion soum.
  • Water sector, 2024–2026: 301 employees committed 243 corruption offences, damage exceeding 700 billion soum (≈$55.5 million).
  • Andijan region (2020): 4.9 billion soum (≈$484,000) spent inefficiently, facilities not completed.

7.2 Conflict of Interest

Current legislation (UP-6074) assigns the operating organization the functions of client for the construction and reconstruction of facilities it operates. In MDB-financed projects under sovereign guarantee, this creates a conflict of interest: the operating organization does not risk its own funds but determines technical decisions and signs acceptance.

Practice: In ADB projects, the functions of client, project management (PIU/PCU), technical supervision, and operation are separated. The operating organization receives the facility only after construction completion and independent acceptance.

Limitation: Simply separating financial and technical functions within one agency does not guarantee independence if they report to the same supervisor. External control mechanisms are required to prevent collusion.

8. Proposed Institutional Model (As an Example, Without Naming Agencies)

8.1 Separation of Functions

  • Financial block — fund movement, compliance with creditor procedures, reporting. Does not approve technical decisions. Signs payments only after technical confirmation.
  • Technical block — engineers hired by competition. Technical specification, technology selection, pipe quality control, hidden works acceptance, pressure testing, batch certification. Has no access to funds.
  • Operating organization — receives the facility after construction completion and independent acceptance.

8.2 "Two-Key" Mechanism

Payment proceeds only with two signatures: the financial block confirms procedural compliance, the technical block confirms quality compliance.

Limitation: If both blocks report to the same supervisor, the mechanism does not work.

8.3 External Control Mechanisms (Mandatory)

  1. Independent technical audit, hired directly by the creditor (MDB), with the right to sample and freeze financing upon detecting non-compliance.
  2. Digital certification (QR coding) of each pipe batch: plant, production date, pressure class, batch number. Eliminates signing of fictitious acceptance acts.
  3. Life-cycle contracts (EPC) with a 10-year warranty: The contractor and designer repair at their own expense. It becomes unprofitable for the contractor to collude on purchasing cheap pipes.

8.4 Technical Block Document Flow (Without Prices)

Technical specification → design documentation → pipe batch incoming inspection act → hidden works acceptance act → facility technical passport.

None of these documents contains prices or amounts. The financial block receives a confirmation register referencing the technical act number. Payment is made only with two confirmations.

9. Proposal: 12 Modular Composite Pipe Plants

9.1 Location Logic

  • 12 regional units (including Karakalpakstan and Tashkent city).
  • Capacity: 100–150 km DN1000 per year per region. National capacity: 1,200–1,800 km/year.
  • With CA network wear at 80%, this scale allows covering a significant share of trunk main replacement needs within 10 years.
  • Local production eliminates long-distance transport and import premiums, reducing the risk of monopoly by a single large plant.

9.2 Technological Requirements

  • Hobas (centrifugal casting) or cross winding on a rigid mandrel — technologies providing predictable ring strength and a sanitarily safe inner layer.
  • Continuous winding remains for gravity systems but is not permitted on pressurized drinking water mains without separate certification.
  • Bell-and-spigot joint with elastomeric seal, tested per ISO 8639.
  • Coupler joint — a source of "play" and leakage, not permitted on trunk mains without confirmed resistance to ground movement.

9.3 Certification and Traceability

  • Each batch — with a QR code (plant, date, pressure class, batch number).
  • An independent laboratory verifies ring stiffness (SN), pressure class (PN), and sanitary migration.
  • Upon detection of non-compliance — the batch is not permitted for installation, and the plant is blacklisted.

11. Conclusion

The fiberglass main failure in Syrdarya is not an isolated "technical glitch." It is the physical projection of the 14,800 fictitiously claimed meters from the Piskent case and 700 billion soum in corruption losses.

Traditional materials in Uzbekistan's conditions fail through documented mechanisms: cast iron cracks under ground movement, steel corrodes and leaks unnoticed, concrete collapses from within, PE migrates organics and has no confirmed service life.

Composite pipes (GRP per ISO 23856, basalt-composite) are the only class of material that simultaneously: does not corrode, is resistant to water hammer and ground movement, is sanitarily safe, has a confirmed 50+ year service life, is repairable, and is already produced in Uzbekistan.

Modular plants by region are not an "experiment" but the scaling of an existing technology to a level where it displaces corruption-friendly but physically unsuitable materials. The saving on a single 130 km project (~$39–44 million) and the annual effect of 12 plants (>$520 million) make construction economically self-sustaining.

"As long as the right to choose the pipe remains with people who bear no personal responsibility for the consequences, the next failure is only a matter of time."
